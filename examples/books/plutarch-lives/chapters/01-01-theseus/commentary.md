Theseus opens the Lives, and Plutarch knows he is starting on the far side of the map: the life begins by comparing itself to the blank edges of a chart, where geographers write that nothing lies beyond but monsters and frozen seas.

So expect legend more than history. Plutarch hopes to bring the myth under “the purifying processes of Reason”: he passes on, for instance, the Cretan story that the Minotaur was really a harsh commander named Taurus and the Labyrinth an ordinary prison. But he keeps the old tales alongside these explanations, and often gives several versions without choosing.

Look for the founder of Athens: Theseus gathering the scattered villages of Attica into one city is the act Plutarch most admires, and it is why he pairs him with Romulus, the founder of Rome.
